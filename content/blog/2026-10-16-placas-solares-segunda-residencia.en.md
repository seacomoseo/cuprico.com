If your villa in Spain spends a good part of the year with the shutters down, and you're wondering whether solar panels on a holiday home make sense when nobody is there to use the electricity, this guide is for you. The same goes if you already have panels and have no clear idea what happens to everything they produce while you're back in the UK, Belgium or France.

The short answer: **solar can work well on a holiday home in Spain, but it shouldn't be designed like a system for a full-time residence.** What matters is not how many weeks you spend there, but what the house consumes while you're away and whether the installation was planned around your calendar. These are the questions owners in our area ask us most, in the order they usually come up.

## "Why would I need solar if the house is empty?"

Because an empty house on the coast is almost never using zero electricity. Picture your villa on a quiet Tuesday in February:

::check:: The pool pump is still filtering, and it is one of the biggest loads in the house.
::check:: Fridge or freezer, router, alarm and cameras run around the clock.
::check:: A dehumidifier, or the air conditioning on dry mode, is common in homes close to the sea to keep damp at bay.
::check:: Irrigation, a well pump or timed outdoor lighting.

Much of that consumption happens during the day, exactly when the panels are producing. That is the first source of savings, and you get it without being there. The second comes when you arrive and usage jumps: air conditioning, heat pump, washing machine, cooking, a full house, perhaps an electric car on charge.

What we won't give you is a fixed savings figure before seeing your bills. Depending on your consumption, your tariff and your calendar, savings can range from modest to very worthwhile.

## "What happens to the energy I produce when I'm not there?"

This is the key question for any holiday-home owner. Whatever the house doesn't use at that moment is exported to the grid. Most homes in Spain use what is called **simplified surplus compensation** (_compensación simplificada_), set out in article 14 of [Royal Decree 244/2019](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2019-5089). In plain terms:

- Your electricity supplier credits the energy you export against your bill, usually at a lower price than you pay for the energy you buy.
- The balance is calculated per billing period, which **cannot be longer than one month**.
- The credit **can never exceed what you paid for grid energy** that month. The energy part of your bill can go down to zero, but not below.
- Standing charges (contracted power), meter rental and the separate taxes are not offset.

The practical consequence: in months when the house is empty and the sun is strong, **some of your surplus won't be credited at all**. That's not a problem in itself, but it is exactly why the system needs to be sized carefully.

Some suppliers offer a so-called "virtual battery" that banks the excess credit in euros for later months. This is not part of the Royal Decree; it is a commercial product, so check the terms closely (monthly fees, minimum contract periods, whether the balance expires). Regulations and tariffs do change, so your own case should always be reviewed before you decide.

## "How big should the system be if I'm only here part of the year?"

This is where a well-planned system differs from an off-the-shelf one. Before we size anything, the first thing we ask is **when you actually use the house**:

| Your calendar | What usually happens | What we focus on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summer stays | ::check:: Peak production meets peak consumption | Air conditioning, pool and a full house: the most rewarding scenario |
| Winter stays | ::check:: Less production per day, but the local sun still delivers | Heating with a heat pump during sunshine hours |
| Occasional visits | ::close:: Risk of oversizing and giving surplus away | Matching the panels to the empty house's base load |
| Planning to move over | ::check:: Consumption, especially at night, will grow | An inverter and consumer unit ready for expansion |

The golden rule: **more panels does not automatically mean more savings.** If half of what they produce for half the year ends up as uncredited surplus, that budget is better spent elsewhere, for example on making the system battery-ready.

Once we know your calendar, we decide how many panels to fit and where. ## "Can I keep an eye on it from abroad?"

Yes. Modern inverters come with a **monitoring app**, so from your phone, wherever you are, you can see:

::check:: How much the panels are producing today, this month and this year.
::check:: How much the house is using and when, which is very useful for spotting something odd while it's empty (a pump that won't stop, air conditioning someone left on).
::check:: How much you are exporting and, if you have a battery, its state of charge.
::check:: Alerts if the inverter detects a fault or loses its connection.

At commissioning we set the app up in your name and, if you wish, keep installer access so we can check the system remotely. If something doesn't look right, you let us know and we look into it. You don't need to book a flight for it.

## "Is it safe to leave it running in an empty house?"

A properly installed system has protection on both the DC and AC sides, and the inverter is required to disconnect automatically if the grid goes down, precisely for safety reasons. You don't need to do anything special when you close the house.

What we do recommend for holiday homes is a **periodic inspection by a qualified installer**, especially near the sea, where salt air is hard on connections and mounting structures. And one rule with no exceptions: never open the consumer unit, switch off the inverter or touch any wiring yourself, and don't ask a neighbour to either. That is our job.

## "How do I make the most of the sun while the house is empty?"

By moving everything that can be scheduled into daylight hours. These are simple settings we configure at commissioning so you don't have to manage anything from abroad:

- **Pool pump** running at midday rather than overnight.
- **Electric water heater or heat pump** heating with the sun.
- **Dehumidifying or ventilation** during production hours.
- **Irrigation** with an electric pump, also during the day.

Take a villa in La Zenia or Villamartín that stays closed from October to June with the pool running: simply moving the pool pump to the middle of the day means much of that consumption is covered by your panels instead of the grid.

## "Is a battery worth it if I'm not there?"

With the house empty, a battery fills up during the day and is barely used at night, so **its financial return drops**. That doesn't mean it never makes sense:

- If you **spend several months in a row** here, a battery lets you use the day's sun at night.
- If you want **backup when the power goes out** (fridge, alarm, router, cameras), a hybrid system with a correctly configured backup function can keep those essential loads running when the grid fails. For an owner keeping watch from another country, that peace of mind counts.
- If you only come for **a few weeks a year**, a battery is usually hard to justify in the short term.

Our usual advice for holiday homes: **start with a battery-ready inverter**, even if you don't fit a battery on day one. If the way you use the house changes, adding one is straightforward. ## What if you end up moving to Spain full time?

It happens more often than people expect: the holiday home becomes the main home at retirement, or once remote working becomes an option. Then the numbers change, because you use more electricity and, above all, more of it at night. That is the moment to add panels, a battery or both, without starting from scratch if the original system was planned properly. That is exactly what we did for an owner who went from a few weeks a year to living here most of it: you can read the [Pinar de Campoverde battery upgrade case study](https://cuprico.com/en/blog/ampliacion-bateria-pinar-de-campoverde/).

A note on tax: in 2026 Spain introduced a new income tax (IRPF) deduction for installing renewable self-consumption systems during the year, under [Royal Decree-law 7/2026](https://sede.agenciatributaria.gob.es/Sede/todas-noticias/2026/marzo/23/medidas-materia-tributacion.html). It is designed for people who pay Spanish IRPF, so if you are tax resident elsewhere it will normally not apply to you. If you become a Spanish tax resident, check the requirements with your tax adviser before counting on it; rules change and every case is different.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I lose the energy my panels produce while I'm away?

Not all of it. Whatever the house uses at that moment (pool, fridge, alarm) is covered by the panels. The rest is exported and, under simplified compensation, credited against that month's bill until the energy charge reaches zero. Anything above that limit isn't credited, unless your supplier offers some form of virtual battery.

### Is solar worth it on a holiday home I only use in summer?

It can be, because your stays coincide with the months of highest production and consumption, and for the rest of the year the panels cover the empty house's base load. The key is not to oversize. We work it out with your actual bills.

### Can I monitor the system from the UK or elsewhere?

Yes. The inverter has a monitoring app showing production, consumption, exports and fault alerts. We set it up in your name and can review it remotely if you ask us to.

### Do I need to be in Spain while the panels are installed?

Not necessarily. We coordinate the survey, installation and paperwork remotely, and only need you for the documents that must be signed.

### Is a battery worth it on a second home?

It depends on how much time you spend here and whether you want backup during power cuts. If you only come for a few weeks, the usual approach is to start without a battery and with an inverter ready to add one later.

### Is it safe to leave the system running in an empty house?

Yes, provided it has been installed properly with the correct protection. We recommend periodic inspections by an installer, especially near the coast. Never handle the consumer unit or inverter yourself.
